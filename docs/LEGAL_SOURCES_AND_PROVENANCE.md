# Legal sources, licenses, and provenance

## Why the full OCGA may be stored

*Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc.*, 590 U.S. 255 (2020) (No. 18-1150): the Supreme Court held
that the annotations in the Official Code of Georgia Annotated are ineligible for copyright under
the government edicts doctrine because they are authored by the Georgia General Assembly (through
the Code Revision Commission) in the course of its legislative duties. The statutory text was
never copyrightable. The whole OCGA — text and annotations — may therefore be copied, stored,
indexed, and displayed.

This replaces earlier project guidance that the OCGA "must not be copied".

## Rules that still apply

- **Provenance on every record.** Store the source (publisher/mirror), release identifier,
  release or as-of date, file URL, retrieval time, and SHA-256 of the stored content.
- **Annotations are not law.** Keep annotations (history, Code Commission and editor's notes,
  cross references, case notes, law reviews, AG opinions) separate from the section text and label
  them as editorial material.
- **A release date is not an effective date, and not proof of currency.** A bulk release reflects
  the Code as of its date; later sessions amend it. Currency stays `unknown` until checked against a
  newer official source. Explicit "Repealed"/"Reserved" headings in the release may be recorded.
- **No invented citations.** Citations come from the source structure (e.g. `O.C.G.A. § 16-11-131`).
- **Third-party terms.** Respect robots.txt, rate limits, and site terms of each source. Case-law
  bulk data from Free Law Project (CourtListener) is published "free of known copyright
  restrictions"; record the bulk file name/date. Court opinions themselves are government edicts.
